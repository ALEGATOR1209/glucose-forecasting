Continuous Glucose Monitors (CGMs)

  • Wearable sensor placed on the skin (upper arm or abdomen)
  • Measures interstitial glucose every 5 min — 288 readings/day
  • Worn continuously for 10–14 days per sensor
  • Used by T1DM, T2DM, and increasingly by healthy individuals
Key output: A dense physiological time series — ideal for ML, but only one modality of what wearables capture.

Cross-Attention (sensor fusion)

  • Glucose signal acts as query
  • HR and steps provide keys/values
  • Model learns when and how much each sensor matters
  • Example: heart rate spike after a meal predicts glucose rise

Multi-Scale Attention (temporal)

  • Fine scale (5 min): rapid glucose fluctuations
  • Medium scale (10 min): meal/activity responses
  • Coarse scale (20 min): longer metabolic trends
  • All three run in parallel and are combined

Clinical context: CGM sensors themselves have ~10% MARD. Sugar I's 8.25% MARD means our predictions are approaching the accuracy of the sensor itself — at 60 minutes into the future.
What these metrics mean: MAE = average error in mg/dL  |  RMSE = penalizes large errors more  |  MARD = % error relative to actual glucose level (lower = better for all)
EXPLAIN the metrics — the audience may not know MAE/RMSE. The KEY insight: our prediction MARD (8.25%) is BETTER than the CGM sensor's own measurement MARD (~10%). That's a powerful soundbite. Mention the improvement over the original GluMind paper results.

Bonus: Learning Without Forgetting

How to train on new patient cohorts without losing accuracy on previous ones

The Problem

Training sequentially on cohorts (Healthy → Pre-T2DM → T2DM) causes catastrophic forgetting: the model forgets earlier cohorts.

This matters for clinical deployment where new hospital data arrives over time.

Our Solution

Knowledge distillation: A frozen "teacher" model preserves old knowledge while a "student" learns new cohort patterns.

L = Ltask + λ · Ldistill

λ = 0.3 balances plasticity (new learning) vs. stability (old knowledge). Result: only ~0.5 mg/dL MAE trade-off vs. global training.

Why this matters clinically: A model deployed in a hospital can be updated with local patient data without retraining from scratch and without losing what it already knows. This enables personalized medicine at scale.

The lambda=0.3 finding and the small 0.5 MAE trade-off are the key numbers. This can be a quick slide — move through it faster if time is tight.

Beyond Glucose: A General-Purpose Architecture

Sugar I's cross-attention + multi-scale design is not glucose-specific. It applies to any multimodal biomedical time series:

ECG + SpO2

Cardiac event prediction from wearable ECG + pulse oximetry

EEG + EMG

Sleep staging from multi-channel neurophysiology

Cortisol + HR + Activity

Stress response prediction from multimodal wearables

The pattern: Whenever you have multiple physiological signals at different sampling rates that carry complementary information, cross-attention can learn to fuse them. The architecture is a recipe, not a one-off solution.
This is important for bioinformaticians — they may work with other biosignal types. Frame Sugar I as a general tool they could apply to their own data. Mention that the codebase is modular: swapping in different input modalities requires minimal code changes (just the embedding layer).

Conclusions & Next Steps

What We Showed

  • Multimodal cross-attention transformers outperform baselines by 40–50% on T2DM
  • Prediction accuracy (8.25% MARD) approaches CGM sensor accuracy
  • Error pattern mirrors disease severity — a biological signal
  • Continual learning enables cohort-incremental training with minimal forgetting
  • Architecture is general-purpose, not glucose-specific
